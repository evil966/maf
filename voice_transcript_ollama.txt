In the world of information systems, since the mid-sixties, the approach taken is topical.

Why?

Because computer science, and the field of information technology, demands that the user must define the requirements.

Unfortunately, in those early years, and even up to now, the guy defining the requirements will inevitably be topical.

He just focuses on his section, right?

And IT has been successful in providing services to this topical user.

Okay?

That is topical thinking.

You look at the problem in front of you. This department. This process. This request.

Now, I want to be fair. Topical thinking is not stupid. Sometimes you need it. If something is on fire, you put out the fire.

The problem is when we stop there.

Of course, a topical solution can still deliver one wish.

Take the banking industry. Just the deposit system. A topical, single obligation.

It provided convenience to the public. It provided speed of processing for the institution.

That is a real result.

But even in banks, it took maybe twenty years to integrate that deposit system with receivable, or with the general ledger.

So we are now in a stage where many companies are still struggling under topical solutions.

One guy would build the inventory system. Another team would build the receivables. Another team would build the general ledger.

It became a fragmented topical adaptation of IT.

Each piece worked.

The company, as a whole, did not.

Now, an information system is not essentially a topical problem.

It is a systemic problem.

Why?

Because a transaction coming from sales will impact many applications.

It hits inventory. It hits receivable. It hits collection. It hits the warehouse. It hits the books.

Systemic thinking, in simple language, means this.

Don't just look at the broken part. Look at the machine that produced the broken part.

If a restaurant's food is always late, don't simply blame the waiter. Look at the kitchen. The inventory. The ordering. The communication.

The waiter is what you see. The restaurant is the system.

So systemic thinking asks a different question.

Not, "What does this user want?"

But, "What else moves when this transaction moves?"

Now, as we went on, becoming systemic was a process of discovery.

In nineteen sixty-eight, I handled a project on raw materials, part and ssupply. We were doing consulting work. Carton and tape, in those days.

Inventory and accounts payable always pushed together. The combination of the two was inevitable.

Out of that, we added cost accounting. Then finished goods. Then accounts receivable.

Take note, ladies and gentlemen. That is the foundation of ERP.

Because what is ERP?

Essentially, it is managing operations on all major resources of the company.

Number one, receivable.

Number two, inventory.

Number three, accounts payable.

Number four, treasury.

The industry, unfortunately, computer science, unfortunately, did not push this.

Other parts of the world continued with their own topical solution.

Until late seventy-nine, nineteen eighty, in Germany, the founders of SAP spun off to sell an inventory system and called it resource management.

Then they sold it in the Netherlands. And Shell saved fifteen million dollars on accounts payable.

You see? Inventory and payable together. That is systemic.

That is why SAP and NetSuite became appealing. In one product, they did accounting, and they did operations.

Up to now, many QuickBooks users cannot really use the subsidiary ledger of the accountant.

Most of the world is still in that mode. Migrating to SAP, NetSuite, and so on.

Believe me, ERP need not be that expensive.

Keep the general ledger online. Keep receivable online to collections. Inventory to the store.

That is topical thinking, running two systems that should be one.

You fund the visible pain. You get a project. You get a module. You get a local success.
